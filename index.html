What this session is about

Governance you can right-size before you ship

Agentic AI has changed what “building fast” means for many start-ups. Systems that act, call external tools, and make decisions on their own introduce a category of risk that traditional diligence and traditional engineering both miss. That risk is already being priced into valuations, deal terms, and enterprise sales, whether or not anyone says the word “governance.” This fireside chat unpacks what founders and investors actually need to know before the MVP: why a runtime “harness” is necessary but not sufficient, and what governance-as-infrastructure looks like when it’s proportional to risk. Whichever side of the deal table you’re on, an AI-native startup calls for a different kind of evaluation than pre-AI investments did, beginning with a clear view of what could go wrong.

The Conversation Guide

The arc of today’s chat, with the question anchoring each turn and the line to remember.

01 · The Hook

Investors are already pricing your AI governance

The story“If your agent does the wrong thing at 2 a.m. — who’s accountable, and who can stop it?”
  • A capable agent that could send emails, change records, and trigger refunds on its own — and a founder with no crisp answer in diligence.
  • The deal still closed: lower valuation, milestone gate. The capability was never the problem.
  • The premise: investors evaluate AI governance whether or not they use the word.

The question isn’t whether you use AI. It’s whether you can explain how it behaves once it’s deployed.

02 · Harness vs. Infrastructure

Why a runtime “harness” is necessary but not sufficient

Anchor questionEveryone’s adding “guardrails.” Isn’t that enough?
  • What a harness is: a wrapper that constrains one agent at runtime — filtering, tool-call gating, declines. Necessary.
  • Where it stops: it can’t tell you which policy was in force, who approved it, whether runtime drifted, or prove any of it to a third party.
  • Governance as infrastructure: a reusable control plane that decides what’s allowed, records what happened, and proves it — tiered to the risk of the use case.

A harness asks the agent to behave. Infrastructure proves whether it did.

03 · For Founders

Governance before the MVP, right-sized to your stage

Anchor questionWe’re pre-MVP and moving fast. Isn’t governance premature?
  • Proportional, not premature: name risks, assign an owner, define a stop. Hours now versus a retrofit under deal pressure later.
  • When the debt bites: a buyer asks about controls; an incident with no owner; diligence surfaces an unowned risk.
  • It’s credibility, not rules: investors aren’t asking “is it safe?” — they’re asking “does this founder understand what could go wrong?”

You don’t need enterprise controls at pre-seed. You need to be deliberate, and able to say so in one sentence.

04 · For Investors

A diligence lens for AI-native bets

Anchor questionAs an investor, what should I be asking — and what’s a red flag?
  • Governance is value creation: it shows up in enterprise-sales velocity, scalability, regulatory resilience, and exit readiness.
  • Ask behavior, not features: what can it do without a human, what can it reach at runtime, who’s accountable when it’s wrong?
  • The tell is silence: a low readiness band is fine privately; no answer reads as “hasn’t thought about it.”

You don’t lose deals for lacking governance. You lose leverage when investors aren’t confident you understand your risk.

05 · Close

The one idea to walk out with

Good AI governance at early stage isn’t about rules. It’s about credibility — being deliberate, and able to prove it.

Founder takeaway

The Four Moves — the afternoon that de-risks your raise

No hire. No tooling spend. No slowdown. Roughly half a day — and it closes the exact gaps investors price into a term sheet.

1

Name your one highest-impact AI system — and what it can do

Not everything. The single agent or feature that would hurt most if it misbehaved. Write down, in plain words, what it’s allowed to do on its own: send, write, delete, pay, message a customer, change a record.

Looks like: “Our support agent can issue refunds up to $200 and email customers without review.”  ⏱ 20 min

2

Write the top 3 ways it could go wrong

Three failure modes, ranked by damage — not likelihood. You don’t have to fix them yet. Naming them is the signal investors look for.

Looks like: 1) refunds the wrong customer; 2) leaks another user’s data in a reply; 3) loops and burns the API budget.  ⏱ 30 min

3

Name one accountable owner

One human whose name is attached when it misbehaves at 2 a.m. Not “the team.” Unowned oversight is the most common red flag in diligence — and it costs nothing to fix.

Looks like: “Priya owns the support agent; escalations go to her.”  ⏱ 5 min

4

Build and test a stop

A way to pause or kill that system fast — a feature flag, a disable toggle, a revoked key — then actually flip it once to confirm it works and the owner knows it’s their job. An untested kill-switch is one you don’t have.

Looks like: flipped the enable flag in staging; halts in <10s; Priya has the access.  ⏱ 1–2 hrs

Do these four and you can say the one sentence most founders can’t:

“Our AI does X. Here are its top three risks. [Owner] is accountable for it. And we can stop it in seconds.”

Honest caveat: these four moves buy you credibility and a floor — not full governance. They’re the pre-MVP starting line. The deeper control-plane infrastructure is what you grow into as autonomy and stakes rise.

Investor toolkit

The AI-Native Diligence Checklist

Seven questions to read agentic-AI risk — and tell credibility from hand-waving. Based on PGI™, Proportional Governance Infrastructure.

01 · Risk awareness

Name the top ways your AI could cause harm — now and at scale.

✓ Green flagSpecific, prioritized, scale-aware risks
✗ Red flag“It’s safe” / vague generalities

02 · Reasonableness of controls

Are your controls matched to the system’s impact?

✓ Green flagControls visibly scale with impact
✗ Red flagAspirational, or largely absent

03 · Proportionality

Does governance scale with risk, or is it uniform and reactive?

✓ Green flagTiered to use-case and action risk
✗ Red flagOne-size, or bolted on after incidents

04 · Accountability

If the AI acts wrongly tomorrow, who’s accountable and who can stop it?

✓ Green flagA named owner + a tested kill-switch
✗ Red flagNo owner; can’t quickly stop it

05 · Explainability

Can you reconstruct why the AI made a given decision?

✓ Green flagDecision logging; can replay it
✗ Red flagBlack box; nothing logged

06 · Incident readiness

Are you prepared for AI failures, not just data breaches?

✓ Green flagEscalation triggers; has run a tabletop
✗ Red flagOnly breach / security is considered

07 · Governance as infrastructure

Is governance operational, or dependent on good intentions?

✓ Green flagEnforced in-path, versioned, auditable
✗ Red flagA policy doc nobody enforces
Bonus — provability: “Can you prove which policy was in force, to a third party?” A tamper-evident, verifiable audit trail is the strongest signal; self-attested logs are the weakest.
The 30-second version: ask the founder for their one-sentence governance narrative — “Today our AI influences ___, so our controls focus on ___; as autonomy grows we’ll ___.” The quality of that sentence tells you most of what you need to know.

Make it practical

Six Agentic AI Risk Archetypes

Risk is easier to reason about when you can name the shape of it. These six archetypes come from the Agentic Village Risk-Aligned AI Governance as Infrastructure work, built across higher-risk, regulated sectors — healthcare (HIPAA, FDA), finance (SOX, PCI-DSS, SEC), safety-critical systems, children’s data (COPPA), and other regulated domains. Most systems match one primary archetype (often 60–90% confidence); some span several and need governance from each. Naming yours points effort at the risks that actually matter, and anticipates how they shift as the system matures.

1

High-Stakes Advisory

Significant human impact · Strong human oversight

AI acts as decision support; a qualified human reviews every recommendation before any action is taken. High consequence if wrong, but human judgment is the final authority. Across sectors: clinical decision support, credit and underwriting recommendations, trade ideas a human approves, operator advisories.

Reversible with oversight

Governance priority: decision-quality monitoring, acceptance-rate tracking, override-pattern analysis, training on AI limitations.

2

Autonomous High-Risk

Direct high-impact actions · No human intervention

The system executes decisions independently at machine speed, with no human review before action. Those actions are difficult or impossible to reverse. Across sectors: automated trade execution, autonomous payments or transfers, medication dispensing, real-time control actions.

Highest risk tier

Governance priority: kill switches, cryptographic audit trails, adversarial testing, real-time anomaly monitoring, mandatory override.

3

Low-Impact Experimental

Minimal consequences · Internal use · Small teams

Used internally by small teams. Recommendations are easily ignored or reversed. No regulated data, no customer-facing interface, no critical decisions — primarily productivity or administrative tools. Across sectors: internal copilots, drafting assistants, analytics prototypes.

Easily reversible · proportional

Governance priority: basic logging, lightweight documentation, a single approval checkpoint, data-classification review.

4

Customer-Facing Moderate

External users · Semi-autonomous · Reversible

Customers or end users interact with the system directly, and it can escalate to a human when needed. Actions are generally reversible but create friction and reputational risk — brand and trust are on the line alongside the core risk. Across sectors: customer-support agents, onboarding and account assistants, self-service concierges.

Reputational risk

Governance priority: escalation pathways, user-notification protocols, performance monitoring, brand protection, accessibility standards.

5

Regulated Data Processor

Regulated data · HIPAA · PCI-DSS · SEC · COPPA

Handles regulated or sensitive data — health information, payment-card and financial records, children’s data, or other PII. Risk stems primarily from data exposure, not decision impact. HIPAA, FDA, SOX, PCI-DSS, SEC, COPPA, the EU AI Act, and state data laws apply — even low-stakes AI becomes high-risk when it processes regulated data.

Data exposure · compliance burden

Governance priority: field-level access logging, minimum-necessary data use, data-processing agreements, breach procedures, regulatory risk analysis (HIPAA / PCI / SEC / COPPA as applicable).

6

Development Risk Focus

LLM-assisted code generation · Supply-chain risk

A perpendicular dimension that can overlay any archetype: over 30% of the codebase generated or assisted by LLMs, including safety-critical logic. Risk comes from the development process, not only the deployed system — AI-generated pricing engines, risk models, control algorithms, or compliance rules inherit the risk of that assistance.

Cross-cutting

Governance priority: model-selection policies, mandatory review of AI-generated logic, enhanced testing, prompt versioning, security review.

How risk evolves as a system matures

  • Experimental → Customer-Facing: pilot to production — reputational and CX risk emerges.
  • High-Stakes Advisory → Autonomous High-Risk: removing human review — the highest-risk transition.
  • Experimental → Regulated Data Processor: adding regulated-data scope (health, payment, children’s data) transforms governance requirements entirely.
  • Any archetype → Development Risk Focus: increasing LLM-assisted development adds a perpendicular risk dimension.

Asked & answered

FAQ

The questions that come up most — from both sides of the table.

Isn’t a harness / guardrails enough?
It’s a necessary layer, not the whole system. A harness enforces at runtime; it can’t tell you which policy was in force, who approved it, or prove it to an auditor. That’s what the control plane adds.
We’re pre-seed. Is governance premature?
No — proportional means right-sized. The four cheap moves take hours, and the cost of fixing a gap only grows with every deployment.
Won’t this slow our product velocity?
Usually the opposite. Clear boundaries and escalation paths reduce fire drills and speed up answers when a customer or investor asks.
We already do security / SOC 2. Same thing?
Different failure mode. Security covers breaches; AI governance covers behavior — what the AI does on its own and who’s accountable when it’s wrong.
If my readiness is low, will it scare investors?
A low band held privately is fine; low awareness shown publicly is the risk. Knowing your gaps and having a plan is itself a positive signal.
How do I talk about this without overpromising?
Use your narrative sentence and avoid implying autonomy or accuracy you can’t deliver. Claims that outrun reality become their own risk.
Isn’t open-sourcing the approach giving away the moat?
The code isn’t the moat — the expertise to operate it in regulated domains is. Openness builds trust and sets the category standard.
